# Why Are We Here?

I am a designer who has already spent years letting machines do the heavy lifting. In undergraduate game demos I typed a few sentences into Midjourney or TripoAI and received usable concept art, motion tests, and even rough 3D assets. In graphic-design courses the same tools filled mood boards in minutes. Last year, while building a digital-twin system for a scenic area, we flew drones, processed oblique-photography data, and generated the base 3D model almost automatically. The efficiency was undeniable. So when systems that write working code from a single English sentence keep improving every six months, why should I, in 2026, still learn to program?

The short answer is that **describing is not the same as specifying**, and specifying remains a human craft.

Anyone can ask for “a generative poster that feels unsettling.” Getting a result that actually unsettles *me*—that fits the exact conceptual, formal, and emotional constraints of a project—is a different skill. When I used AI image generators for game demos, the first outputs were often beautiful but wrong: wrong lighting, wrong cultural reference, wrong emotional temperature. The difference between those outputs and the ones I finally kept was not luck. It was the precision of the language I learned to use, the ability to iterate on parameters, and, more importantly, the ability to recognise when the machine had misunderstood the brief. That recognition required a mental model of how the system works.

The same pattern appears with code. If I cannot read what the machine produces, I cannot tell whether it is fragile, over-engineered, or quietly solving the wrong problem. Reading is not optional once writing becomes cheap. Describing (“make me a system that does X”) is easy. Specifying—deciding exactly what X means, what constraints matter, what trade-offs are acceptable, and whether the result actually delivers—is the hard part. That hard part is still design work.

Don Ihde’s argument about mediated perception is useful here. Technologies are never neutral tools; they reshape what we notice as possible. A painter who does not understand paint will eventually be limited by the material rather than liberated by it. The same is true of code and of generative systems. When I treated AI image generators as pure black boxes, my creative range narrowed to whatever the model already did well. Once I began treating the prompt as a programmable interface—adjusting seeds, weights, negative prompts, control nets—the medium expanded again. Programming literacy is the difference between being a passenger and being a pilot of the same system.

This is not a new problem. In 1965 Frieder Nake and Georg Nees were already being accused of building machines that would replace artists. Photography was supposed to end painting; synthesisers were supposed to end musicianship. What actually happened is that each new medium forced the older practices to become more deliberate about their own strengths. The skill that survived was not the ability to execute every mark by hand, but the ability to decide what was worth making and to judge whether the result was any good.

Ada Lovelace already understood this distinction in the 1840s. In her notes on Babbage’s Analytical Engine she insisted that the machine could only do what it was precisely instructed to do, yet she also recognised that the same mechanism opened possibilities its inventor had not fully imagined. The skill that mattered was the ability to see both the limits and the latent potential of the medium. That skill is still required. When I directed the oblique-photography pipeline for the digital-twin project, the technical process was largely automated. The design decisions—which level of detail mattered for visitor experience, how the model should respond to different scales of interaction, where the twin should remain deliberately incomplete—were not. Those decisions required an understanding of both the data and the systems that would later consume it.

There is an uncomfortable possibility that much of the specific syntax taught in a course like this will look obsolete within a decade. I have already experienced versions of that obsolescence during my undergraduate studies. What I do not accept is that the underlying capacity—the ability to think in systems, to specify intent with precision, and to judge the quality of what a machine returns—will become obsolete. That capacity is closer to design literacy than to any particular programming language. Learning to program now is less about future-proofing a technical skill and more about refusing to outsource the part of the practice that still belongs to the designer: the part that decides what is worth making and whether the result is any good.

If the machine writes the code, someone still has to know whether it is the right code, for the right reasons, in the right form. I would rather be that someone.
